# PFAS Market & Regulatory Landscape

Every number below has a source. Where I couldn't verify something (including the $132B figure that showed up in an earlier draft of this brief), I left it out rather than repeat it.

## Regulatory status (verified via epa.gov, checked 2026-08-15)

The EPA finalized a National Primary Drinking Water Regulation for PFAS on April 10, 2024, the first enforceable federal limit on these chemicals in drinking water.

- **PFOA and PFOS**: 4.0 parts per trillion (ppt) maximum contaminant level
- **PFHxS, PFNA, and HFPO-DA**: 10 ppt each
- **PFAS mixtures**: Hazard Index of 1

Water systems have to start monitoring by 2027 and hit compliance by 2029. The EPA itself projects the rule will "prevent PFAS exposure in drinking water for approximately 100 million people" over time. $1 billion in funding was made available through the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act to help states and private well owners cover remediation.

## Cost figures (verified via a peer-reviewed review, PMID 40203642, "PFAS regulations and economic impact: A review of U.S. pulp & paper and textiles industries")

These are the real numbers I could find and cite:

- Wastewater treatment costs across affected industries projected to reach **$3 billion annually**
- Healthcare costs tied to PFAS-linked diseases estimated to **exceed $62 billion**
- At least one documented settlement: a paper mill settling for **$11.9 million** over historical PFAS pollution

The same paper also mentions "estimates exceeding the global GDP of $106 trillion." That's not included as a real figure here. It reads like a rhetorical extreme used to illustrate how large worst-case totals could theoretically get under certain assumptions, not a serious standalone estimate, and repeating it out of context would be misleading.

## What this actually tells you

There's real regulatory pressure (hard 2029 deadline), real documented cost ($3B/year treatment, $62B+ healthcare), and real settlement precedent. That's enough to establish "this is a real, expensive problem" without needing a single splashy headline number. Whether it adds up to a market a small team can meaningfully address is a separate question this data doesn't answer.
